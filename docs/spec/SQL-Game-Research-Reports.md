# A Detective Game That Grades Queries, Not Names

**Every SQL learning game in the market validates an answer string, not the SQL that produced it — and that single architectural choice is the unoccupied position a new game should take.** SQL Murder Mystery grades you with a hardcoded SQLite `AFTER INSERT` trigger that hex-matches two names (`4A6572656D7920426F77657273` → "Jeremy Bowers"; `4D6972616E6461205072696573746C79` → "Miranda Priestly"), which buys it extraordinary portability and costs it every feature people ask for: no hints, no progress saving, no per-query feedback, and an answer recoverable with one `SELECT sql FROM sqlite_master WHERE type='trigger'`. SQL Noir, the state of the art, has independently shipped nearly every improvement on the obvious gap list — six cases across 1–5 difficulty tiers, XP, server-side answers for paid cases, rate limiting at 30 attempts per 60 seconds per IP, dual-mode progress persistence, three locales — and yet its `/api/check-solution` still compares a submitted name string, and grepping its case-study components for "hint" returns nothing. Meanwhile the learning-science evidence points squarely at what both are missing: only **~6% of student query executions are correct**, **54% carry syntax errors**, **51% of unsuccessful students abandon a question after failing to fix a syntax error**, and the errors that persist into final wrong answers are overwhelmingly *omissions* — missing expression at 0.29 overall and **0.65 in self-joins**, extraneous or omitted grouping column at 0.18 rising to **0.40 in aggregate queries**. A new game wins not by writing a better murder but by diagnosing the query: PGlite 0.5.13 puts **real PostgreSQL 18.6** in the browser for ~5.1 MB gzipped with no COOP/COEP headers required, and libpg-query at **338 KB gzipped** adds Postgres's own parser for structural feedback at 7% of the engine's size. That combination — real Postgres execution plus authoritative AST analysis, both offline, both on a static host — is technically available today and commercially unoccupied.

## SQL Murder Mystery's entire design follows from putting the grader inside the data file

The game is six inference hops through one SQLite database, bootstrapped by prose on the page rather than data in the DB. The player is told three facts — a **murder**, on **Jan. 15, 2018**, in **SQL City** — and taught exactly two bootstrap queries, both SQLite-specific introspection against `sqlite_master`, before the page's next heading reads "The rest is up to you!" ([SQL Murder Mystery](https://mystery.knightlab.com/)). The chain runs: filter `crime_scene_report` on type, city and date; identify two witnesses via `ORDER BY address_number` on one street and `LIKE 'Annabel%'`; read their transcripts for the "48Z" gym membership prefix, gold status, and the "H42W" plate fragment; join `get_fit_now_member` → `get_fit_now_check_in` → `person` → `drivers_license` to isolate Jeremy Bowers; then an epilogue that reads Bowers' own transcript and triangulates the mastermind through `GROUP BY ... HAVING COUNT(*)=3` over a three-table join. An educator write-up independently confirms the two-query epilogue challenge as the memorable capstone ([University of Illinois Scholarly Commons](https://publish.illinois.edu/commonsknowledge/?p=5837)).

The ceiling is low and structurally so. Direct inspection of the shipped `sql-murder-mystery.db` at HEAD commit `a7aa227` (2025-04-14) confirms nine tables and ~56,000 rows in 3,661,824 bytes: `person` 10,011 rows, `drivers_license` 10,007, `facebook_event_checkin` 20,011, `income` 7,514, `interview` 4,991, `get_fit_now_check_in` 2,703, `crime_scene_report` 1,228, `get_fit_now_member` 184, plus an empty `solution` scratch table. **`HAVING COUNT(*)=3` on a three-way join is the hardest construct in the whole game** — no subqueries, CTEs, window functions, outer joins, `UNION` or `CASE` are required anywhere, and the walkthrough's own closing line before the finale is "there's no other syntax that you need!" All dates are `INTEGER` in `YYYYMMDD` form, which is why `BETWEEN 20171201 AND 20171231` works for "December 2017" and why no real date function ever appears — a pedagogical liability, since learners meet `DATE`/`TIMESTAMP` columns and `DATE_TRUNC`/`EXTRACT` in every real warehouse. Only two indexes exist, both auto-created by SQLite for text primary keys, so every lookup is a table scan and the game teaches nothing about plans.

Grading lives in the data file, not in an application, and that is the cleanest single insight of the teardown. Because the trigger is inside the SQLite artifact, the same `.db` grades you in SQLiteStudio, a Jupyter notebook, Datasette, or the browser — portability for free, at the cost of being unable to grade anything except a final string, unable to see the player's query, unable to hide the answer, and unable to persist state. Matching is `hex()` equality with no `LOWER()` or `TRIM()`, so "jeremy bowers" fails. Grepping `index.html`, `walkthrough.html` and `scripts/main.js` for `localStorage`/`sessionStorage` returns zero matches: a reload re-downloads the DB and discards everything. Hints exist only as `data-solution` attributes on **2 of roughly 15** walkthrough exercises, and the walkthrough's own copy concedes its verdict is unreliable, telling players that if they wrote a different-but-correct query they should "disregard the word 'incorrect'." The brute-force surface is wide open — unlimited attempts, no rate limit, and the answers readable from the trigger DDL — which is exactly the still-open issue **#18 "Possible to see solution before starting mystery," opened Dec 21, 2019** ([NUKnightLab/sql-mysteries issues](https://github.com/NUKnightLab/sql-mysteries/issues)). Universities paper over this externally: Dartmouth CS 61 binds students by honor code, requires a commented `.sql` file, and grades out of **5 points — 3 for the killer, 2 for who paid** ([Dartmouth CS 61 Lab 1](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/~tjp/cs61/labs/lab1/index.html)).

The stack is deliberately primitive and that is partly a virtue. Two hand-written HTML pages, five vendored JS files, no `package.json`, no build step. `scripts/worker.sql.js` is **2,721,851 bytes of sql.js in its asm.js, pre-WASM form** — `grep -c wasm` returns 0 — loaded into a Web Worker while the 3.6 MB `.db` is pulled by `XMLHttpRequest` with `responseType='arraybuffer'` and held entirely in memory before the submit buttons are enabled. Vendored lodash (539,838 bytes) and CodeMirror (374,262 bytes) add roughly 900 KB for what a 10,683-byte `main.js` uses. The consequences are logged and unfixed: **#26 "Database takes a long time to initialize on Chrome" (Apr 16, 2021)** and **#47 "the project isn't fully offline-friendly" (Sep 28, 2025)**, the latter caused by hard dependencies on unpkg and cdn.knightlab.com ([issues list](https://github.com/NUKnightLab/sql-mysteries/issues)). The exact sql.js version could not be pinned — the vendored file carries no version banner and has never been touched since its initial commit — so "asm.js, pre-WASM" is all that is established.

Adoption is broad precisely because the artifact is institutionally frictionless, not because the game is good. GitHub reports **2.2k stars, 311 forks, 15 open issues**, up from **1,785 stars / 251 forks** in a March 2025 metadata snapshot — roughly **+415 stars and +60 forks in 19 months on a repo whose current HEAD is a merged typo fix** ([repo page](https://github.com/NUKnightLab/sql-mysteries/); [ecosyste.ms](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/api/v1/projects/github.com%2FNUKnightLab%2Fsql-mysteries)). It is Lab 1 at Dartmouth on MySQL, a rated-intermediate ~2-hour tutorial in the Galaxy Training Network shipped as two Jupyter notebooks ([Galaxy Training](https://training.galaxyproject.org//topics/data-science/tutorials/sql-game/tutorial.html)), indexed in ELIXIR's TeSS registry ([TeSS](https://tess.elixir-europe.org/materials/hands-on-for-sql-educational-game-murder-mystery-tutorial)), programmed at public libraries, link-blogged by Simon Willison on Oct 7, 2019 and loaded straight into Datasette ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2019/Oct/7/sql-murder-mystery-datasette)), covered by Boing Boing two days later ([Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2019/10/09/murder-in-sql-city.html)), and catalogued as a game on RAWG ([RAWG](https://rawg.io/games/sql-murder-mystery)). A librarian or TA hands out one URL or one 3.6 MB MIT/CC-licensed file with no accounts, no install, no cost and no privacy review, and it self-grades. **That frictionlessness is the hardest thing about SMM to beat, and any competitor requiring login or payment forfeits most of this distribution channel on day one.** No player-count or traffic figures exist publicly — GA measurement ID `G-8F4WPDMPL5` collects them but Knight Lab has published nothing, so stars and forks are the only quantitative proxies. Bootcamp *curriculum* adoption is strongly implied by write-up volume but no primary syllabus was located; treat it as unverified.

Two predicted criticisms did not survive contact with evidence. The hypothesised "it's SQLite not Postgres" grievance is **absent** — zero Postgres or alternate-dialect requests across all 15 open issues, and courses wanting another engine simply re-host (Dartmouth on MySQL, without remarking on it). The real portability defect is narrower: `sqlite_master` introspection, which the site itself flags as non-portable, and `YYYYMMDD` integers instead of date types. And "no beginner onboarding" is only half true: there *is* a walkthrough page with ~15 graded exercises, `prompt_beginner.pdf`, a 478 KB `reference.pdf` crash course, and an install guide — the defensible criticism is that this is a separate static document rather than an adaptive in-game ramp, with specific holes, most notably that an educator working through the joins section found no warning that joining tables with identically-named columns causes errors ([Illinois](https://publish.illinois.edu/commonsknowledge/?p=5837)). The criticism that actually matters is the frozen-supply pattern: growing adoption against 15 open issues spanning seven years, two unfixed data-integrity bugs in the critical puzzle path (**#39 dangling SSNs**, Jun 29, 2023; **#44 villain's height possibly mismatching `drivers_license`**, Jul 5, 2025), translation requests open since 2019 and 2020, `requirements.txt` still pinning JupyterLab 1.x, and a footer reading 2019. **A competitor does not need to out-design SMM so much as out-maintain it.** Hacker News and most Reddit threads were unreadable in this research, so community sentiment on r/SQL and r/dataengineering remains uncharacterised.

## SQL Noir fixed progression and left query-level feedback untouched

SQL Noir is the benchmark, verified by cloning `hristo2612/SQLNoir` at HEAD dated **2026-10-28** (latest commit: "Recover pending solves after reload"). The stack is Next.js 14.2.35, React 18.3.1, TypeScript 5.5, Tailwind 3.4, **sql.js ^1.10.2 in its modern WASM build**, Supabase for auth and progress, Stripe ^20.4.1 for payments, PostHog plus Vercel Analytics, reactflow ^11.11.4 for an interactive schema diagram, `next-intl` ^4.8.3, and Vitest ^4.1.0. There is no shipped `.db` at all: each case's database is **a TypeScript array of `CREATE TABLE` + `INSERT` statements** executed into a fresh in-memory sql.js instance on load, dynamically imported per locale from `src/cases/schemas/{locale}/{caseId}.ts`. **That TS-array-of-INSERTs case format is the single most copyable idea in the entire competitive set** — it makes a case a reviewable text file, enables PR-based community authoring and per-locale forks, and removes the binary blob, at the cost of raw dataset scale.

Six cases carry explicit difficulty and XP: The Vanishing Briefcase (difficulty 1, beginner, 50 XP), The Stolen Sound (1, beginner, 100), The Miami Marina Murder (2, intermediate, 200), The Midnight Masquerade Murder (5, advanced, 300), The Silicon Sabotage (5, advanced, 1000), The Vanishing Diamond (2, intermediate, 250). The `Case` type declares `difficulty: 1|2|3|4|5` and `category: "beginner"|"intermediate"|"advanced"|"expert"` plus an `Achievement` interface. Schema complexity scales with difficulty — case-001 has 3 tables, case-002 adds a fourth, case-003 and case-006 reach 6, case-004 reaches **9** (`phone_records`, `vehicle_registry`, `catering_orders`, `hotel_checkins`, `surveillance_records`, `witness_statements`, `final_interviews`, `person`, `crime_scene`), and case-005 drops `crime_scene` entirely for a corporate-espionage framing built on keycard, facility, computer and email logs. Each case carries structured content SMM lacks completely: a narrative `brief`, an explicit **`objectives` array** of step-by-step goals, and a `solution` object with `answer`, `successMessage` and a prose **`explanation`** of the reasoning path shown after solving. Datasets are far smaller than SMM's — roughly 700, 500, 1,013, 1,504, 1,200 and 803 inserted tuples for cases 001–006, against SMM's ~56,000 — which buys instant load and reviewable content at the price of haystack.

The anti-cheat and persistence work is genuinely better. `ALWAYS_FREE_CASE_IDS` covers cases 001–003 plus all beginner-category cases; cases 4–6 need a Stripe license, and crucially **paid cases' answers live server-side in `src/cases/solutions.server.ts`** with an in-repo comment that the real solution "lives server-side ... so it is never shipped to the browser" — a direct architectural fix for SMM's readable trigger. `POST /api/check-solution` rate-limits **30 attempts per 60 seconds per IP**, with the in-code rationale that this "throttles automated answer-guessing while leaving normal play untouched," returning 429 plus `Retry-After`; matching is trimmed and case-insensitive. Anonymous players get `localStorage` progress with an explicit documented trust model ("localStorage is fully client-controlled, so we treat [it as untrusted]"), logged-in players get Supabase, and `/api/migrate-progress` merges the two at signup. Localisation runs to three languages against SMM's zero: English, Português (Brasil) and 简体中文, the last rebuilt wholesale by **Lloyd Hasson** (GitHub `SatyrFrost`), a teacher in China who localised character names, moved the setting to Shanghai, and used **Chinese-named SQL tables and columns**, then donated the work ([SQLNoir README](https://github.com/hristo2612/SQLNoir)). It also ships an interactive reactflow schema viewer, an in-game `CaseNotes` scratchpad, a dashboard, confetti, `CONTRIBUTING.md`, a `case-template.ts`, Supabase migrations, a Vitest suite, and a 27-post SEO blog covering CTEs, window functions, join types, HAVING vs WHERE, COALESCE and more.

**The remaining gap in both games is identical: neither grades the query, only the answer.** SQL Noir's endpoint compares a name string exactly as SMM's trigger does. Neither inspects the SQL. No hint system exists in SQL Noir either. And the second unoccupied position follows from the first: SQL Noir's *blog* teaches CTEs and window functions while its *cases* have the same shallow-schema character as SMM, with 500–1,500 rows and no evidence of puzzles that mechanically require window functions, recursive CTEs, `LEFT JOIN` + `IS NULL` absence reasoning, or set operations. A game whose puzzles *require* intermediate SQL, rather than teaching it in prose beside the game, would be new. Live sqlnoir.com state could not be verified in this research — pricing, the current case list, player counts, and whether monetisation is switched on (the code says "when monetization is on") are all unknown, as are its star and fork counts, so no numeric traction comparison against SMM's 2.2k/311 is possible.

Only one project has attempted query-level feedback, and it is not public. A USM project by **Dr. Jasy Liew Suet Yan and Lim Yin Wen** extends the concept with an engine that matches a player's query against stored template solutions and emits targeted corrective hints when it does not match; players advance only on a correct *query*, earn a badge for identifying the murderer, and the work spans **three storylines of 17–23 SQL puzzles each**, mapped to a CLO2-PLO2 psychomotor-skills outcome and winning a **Gold medal and Top 10 Best Projects at PIXEL 2021** ([USM project PDF](https://intelec.usm.my/phocadownloadpap/kategori2-staf/bahan-pdp/SQL%20Murder%20Mystery%20A%20fun%20game%20to%20learn%20SQL%20with%20AI-generated%20hints%20and%20feedback%20-%20Jasy%20Liew%20Suet%20Yan.pdf)). That SQL Noir and the USM project independently converged on the same two additions — per-query feedback and multiple cases with progression — is strong triangulated evidence about which gaps educators actually feel.

## The wider field proves linear story spines teach well and retain badly

Across the rest of the genre, story–mechanic coupling splits three ways. **Authored linear coupling** (Lost at SQL, SQL Island, SQL Escape Room) makes each story beat motivate exactly one new clause, which produces a legible ramp and an unreplayable, unreorderable game. **Procedural decoration** (SQLPD, SQL Squid Game, most gamified courses) wraps an interchangeable fiction around a generated or banked query spec — SQLPD's cases are randomly generated, and its maker's answer to a thin-story complaint was that the story gets more interesting as the queries get harder, i.e. story richness is gated on concept difficulty rather than driving it ([Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/posts/sql-police-department)). **Simulation-as-story** is Schemaverse alone, a space strategy game "implemented entirely inside PostgreSQL" where each player is a database role and SQL *is* the control interface, with no puzzle layer at all ([Schemaverse GitHub](https://github.com/abstrct/schemaverse)).

Lost at SQL has the most concrete difficulty ramp found anywhere in this research, and it reads like a syllabus: 20 chapters with 2 endings, running select-one-column → multiple columns → `SELECT *` → `WHERE` equality → `WHERE` with `AND` → `OR`/`NOT` → `GROUP BY`/`COUNT`/aliases → multi-column grouping → `SUM`/`MAX` → `ORDER BY` → `CASE` → **CTEs at chapter 12** → `JOIN` → `LEFT JOIN` + `IS NULL` → `GROUP_CONCAT`/`FULL OUTER JOIN`/`LIKE` → pattern matching → `ROW_NUMBER()` → date functions, `IN` subqueries, `DISTINCT`, windowed `COUNT` → `UNION ALL`, `CAST`, `REPLACE`, `HAVING` → and an ending that is itself a SQL choice between `SELECT` and a dated `DELETE` ([LearnSQL full solution](https://learnsql.com/blog/lost-at-sql-full-solution/)). Note it deliberately puts `GROUP BY` at chapter 7, *before* `ORDER BY` at 10 and `JOIN` at 13. Its scaffolding is the best-documented three-tier system in the set: a per-chapter **"Learn" button** explaining the concept, hints that **lower your final score** against a leaderboard, a **"Slide to get the answer"** escape hatch recorded as a skip in player stats, and a "Get step queries" button that reloads earlier step queries so multi-step chapters do not require retyping ([i-programmer](https://i-programmer.info/news/150-training-a-education/16316-lost-at-sql-the-game.html)). Its engine is unnamed by every source; the i-programmer reviewer infers SQLite from `strftime` and `GROUP_CONCAT` and labels that an inference, which sits awkwardly with chapter 15's `FULL OUTER JOIN` (SQLite only gained it in 3.39, 2022). Its maintenance status and business model are undocumented everywhere.

SQL Island supplies the only quantitative completion data in the entire space, and it argues against the standard assumption about where beginners quit. In a study of **56 students (39 male / 17 female**, mostly second-year undergraduates, compulsory Web Programming course, online during lockdown), **51 of 56 (91%) completed the game** versus 53–80% correct submissions on the course's other assignments, with a mean playing time of **59 minutes**; four finishers spent 2–5 hours. The five non-finishers averaged **84 minutes** and stalled at **10, 10, 15, 17 and 19 of 20 queries** — abandonment clustered at the halfway mark and in the final quarter, in aggregate/JOIN/DML territory, not at `SELECT` ([Xinogalos & Satratzemi, Applied Sciences 12(13):6563](https://mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/13/6563)). Two caveats must travel with that 91%: it was a graded compulsory assignment following two SQL labs, so it is not a free-play completion rate, and on the MEEGA+ **focused attention** dimension students were merely *neutral* about losing track of time — the game did not produce flow even while perceived learning scored a median of 4. The same students asked for better error messages, clearer hints, a save point and the ability to revisit earlier stages, because SQL Island is **stateless** — quit mid-game and you restart. A database academic's structural criticism is harsher and generalises to the whole linear-narrative genre: SQL Island is a "strictly sequential series of exercises wrapped in a story," with no player freedom and no player-specific playthrough ([Brass, 14 Feb 2025](https://users.informatik.uni-halle.de/~brass/db24/sql_lernspiel.pdf)). Sources also conflict on its level count — 8 quests, 21 steps, or 20 SQL challenges depending on the source — most likely different granularities of the same game, but no source reconciles them.

Validation strategies across the field fall into four kinds, each with a documented failure. **Result-set comparison** (SQL Squid Game's 9 levels; SQLPD) is hard to cheat but produces ordering false negatives — a SQLPD player's "Unexpected rows" error was diagnosed as a wrong `ORDER BY` making `LIMIT` return the wrong rows ([Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/posts/sql-police-department)). **Type-in-the-value** (SQL Escape Room: MegaCorp, 10 rooms, case-insensitive, with progressively more detailed hints on failure) never inspects the query at all, so a walkthrough value substitutes for writing SQL ([ksshah.github.io](https://ksshah.github.io/sql-escape-room/)). **No validation**, where the database state *is* the outcome: Schemaverse players `INSERT` to buy ships and `UPDATE` to set courses, with the universe advancing in roughly one-minute "tics" ending in a `NOTIFY tic` ([Schemaverse PLAYING.md](https://github.com/abstrct/schemaverse/blob/master/docs/PLAYING.md)). And **cryptographic token derivation from the query itself**, which is SQLab's genuinely novel design: each question has a formula such as `salt_042(sum(hash) OVER ())` that you append to your `SELECT` clause, and a correct query yields a token that unlocks the next episode while an incorrect one can return a tailored hint, because the wrong hash decrypts a hint row ([Ecosyste.ms SQLab](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Flaowantong%2Fsqlab)). SQLab therefore makes a game distributable as a plain database dump with no hosting, no app and no recoverable answer key — exactly what an instructor needs for unsupervised homework — and its instructors can log student queries and accrete new hints over time, so hints improve as a function of real student errors rather than designer imagination. **That is the most teacher-friendly feedback loop found anywhere in this research, and it has one GitHub star.** No game found in this research parses the query to check that the intended construct was used; every system will accept a correct answer obtained by a wrong method.

Commercial evidence says production values, not SQL depth, convert. **Database Detective: Minor Crimes Division** (released 17 July 2026, solo developer Thomas Hsu, $14.99) holds **97% positive across 1,069 all-time reviews and 97% of 137 recent reviews**, ships 10 cases each longer than the last, and offers the most elaborate scaffolding in any commercial product here — a sub-20-page in-game SQL textbook, error messages that point to where a query went wrong, and an in-fiction assistant ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3950130/Database_Detective_Minor_Crimes_Division?l=swedish)). Its criticisms are instructive: a steep curve for newcomers, text-heavy instruction, data that is too clean, **a crash on Linux after 50–60 minutes that can wipe save files**, long unskippable hint animations, no hints for the final query, and no editor autocomplete; completion-time claims also conflict, with the store saying 8–10 hours against aggregated player data suggesting ~20 ([VaporLens](https://vaporlens.app/app/3950130/database_detective_minor_crimes_division.md)). SQLPD's opposite reception — "only covers the basics," "not worth $20," advanced users wanting optimisation challenges — and, worse, **progress saving locked behind the $20 subscription**, so an abruptly ended session erases everything ([Medium review](https://medium.com/illumination/level-up-your-sql-skills-with-this-addictive-online-game-fdb76c8d9ed4)), together make the point that for a multi-hour linear SQL game **save integrity is a pedagogical feature, not an engineering detail**. SQLPD's and Database Detective's complaints also expose the genre's central tension: a story that must be read in order cannot serve both beginners and experienced users, because the entry ramp and the ceiling are the same object.

Maintenance in late 2026 is thin. Only Schemaverse (442 stars, 51 forks, 128 commits, targeting PostgreSQL 19 with CI on 18, sqitch migrations, a Go ticker, a SvelteKit web UI, pgTAP tests, a 2026 copyright footer and a documented one-hour-round classroom preset) and Database Detective show hard dated 2026 activity. SQL Island remains online with no update signal, developed by Johannes Schildgen at RPTU Kaiserslautern with five community translations, while Schildgen himself has moved to OTH Regensburg ([OTH Regensburg](https://www.oth-regensburg.de/personen/detailansicht/johannes-schildgen)) — a frozen game on a former institution's domain is the classic precondition for silent link rot. SQLPD has produced nothing public since its 3 May 2020 launch. SQLab was created March 2024 and last pushed October 2024 with one star. **Live HTTP verification failed for every game site in this session**, so no "live as of" claim here is a confirmed uptime check. Querium, "SQLGame" and "The SQL Game" were searched for and **no evidence was found that they exist as SQL learning games**.

From non-SQL programming games, four lessons transfer with better provenance than most of the SQL corpus. Human Resource Machine starts with **two instructions** and adds one mechanic at a time; TIS-100 escalates steeply but keeps everything solvable and solves the motivation problem by *framing* puzzles as recovering corrupted memory from an inherited machine, which the same article contrasts with Human Resource Machine's story doing "little to keep players going"; Shenzhen I/O rewards domain expertise without requiring it ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/three-computer-games-that-make-assembly-language-fun)). And Flexbox Froggy's Thomas Park supplies the sharpest selection criterion available — he chose flexbox because its properties "make for good gameplay mechanics" — plus the most actionable instrumentation gap: he tracks each level's success rate and the incorrect answers submitted, hoping to identify common misconceptions ([thomaspark.co](https://thomaspark.co/?p=11108)). **Not one SQL game found publishes or appears to use per-level wrong-answer data to improve its hints.** Park's criterion also predicts where linear SQL games go wrong: `GROUP BY`/`HAVING` makes a fine mechanic (counting villagers, finding the strongest village), but window functions, CTEs and `CAST`/`REPLACE` — which Lost at SQL stacks into chapters 17–19 — have no natural gameplay analogue, which is plausibly why late chapters read as exercises with story attached.

## Learners quit at syntax errors and never fix what they omitted

The error research is the best-evidenced material in this entire brief and it should drive the design more than any competitive feature list. In PostgreSQL-logged student attempts, **54% of all executions produced a syntax error, ~40% ran but were semantically wrong, and ~6% were correct** — and **51% of unsuccessful students abandoned a question after failing to fix a syntax error** ([Ahadi et al., SIGCSE 2016](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/41087/1/SIGCSE2016.pdf)). Per query type, failure rates were: simple one-table 89% (54% syntax), `GROUP BY` 93% (68% syntax), `GROUP BY` with `HAVING` 96% (61%), natural join 94% (66%), simple subquery 92% (64%), **self-join 98% (only 38% syntax)**, correlated subquery 93% (55%). The pattern matters: `GROUP BY` has the highest syntax share, while **self-joins fail on thinking, not typing**. The commonest Postgres error codes overall were 42601 syntax error (34,504 occurrences, 21%), 42703 undefined column (20,689, 13%), 42803 grouping error (15,442, 10%), 42P01 undefined table (5,548, 3%) and 42702 ambiguous column (4,844, 3%) — and the profile shifts sharply by task: 42803 is **26% of `GROUP BY` errors and 29% of `GROUP BY`+`HAVING` errors** against 13% in simple one-table queries, while 42703 dominates join and subquery work at 38%, 33% and 32%.

The persistent errors — those remaining in incorrect *final* queries, n=3,739 — are the pedagogically critical set, and they are dominated by omission rather than commission. Across all queries: **missing expression 0.29, extraneous or omitted grouping column 0.18, missing join 0.10, missing column from ORDER BY 0.10, incorrect comparison operator or value 0.08, omitting a join 0.06.** The subsets sharpen it: in multi-table queries (n=2,869) missing expression 0.31 and grouping column 0.22; in equal subqueries (n=156) extraneous expression 0.21 and **missing join 0.17**; in self-joins (n=750) **missing expression 0.65** and grouping column 0.26; in multiple-source-table queries (n=807) **grouping column 0.31**, missing column from SELECT 0.17, omitting a join 0.15; and in aggregate-function queries (n=1,486) **extraneous or omitted grouping column 0.40** and missing expression 0.34 ([Taipalus, "Explaining Causes Behind SQL Query Formulation Errors"](https://aic-atlas.s3.eu-north-1.amazonaws.com/projects/e7299991-eb2b-4764-a849-4909e01fb07d/documents/SLiI3LneAuYdV7P1rSfCPh8wmUpjAnqXklp6B67D.pdf)). Taipalus maps these onto Smelcer's four cognitive accounts — missing expression to working-memory overload in multi-table queries but to *absence of a retrieval cue* in self-joins and aggregates; missing join to absence of retrieval cue; improper subquery nesting to procedural fixedness; most grouping-column errors to plain ignorance — while flagging the mappings as speculative. The taxonomy itself runs to 4 error classes, 18 categories and **105 individual errors**, and logical errors plus "complications" are the most persistent classes, hypothesised to be rooted in fundamental misconceptions rather than slips ([arXiv 2410.16120](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.16120)).

Measured misconception prevalence adds a complementary list, from two questions and roughly 500 answers: `is` (believing `IS` and `==` are valid equality syntax) **28%**, `scoping_with` (a CTE's name need not appear in `FROM`) **26%**, `pk_distinct` (`DISTINCT` is needed on primary keys) **24%**, `eq_pk` (unnecessary comparisons on keys) **23%**, `scoping_selfjoin` (one copy of a table suffices to compare rows) **14%**, `missing_alias` **13%**, `brackets` (`WHERE` must be parenthesised) **11%**, `distinct` (`DISTINCT` takes the first item of a list) **11%**, `neq` (`≠` is valid) **6%**, `understanding_pk` **5%**, `alias_syntax` **1%** ([Miedema et al., Koli Calling 2023](https://daphnemiedema.nl/assets/documents/Koli_Calling2023.pdf)). Two caveats must survive into the spec: the `missing_join` result at 52% is one **the authors instruct be disregarded** due to question ambiguity, and the paper is internally inconsistent about whether these percentages are shares of *answers* or of the *population*. A separate expert Delphi study counted how many distinct causal hypotheses experts proposed per error — a proxy for how multi-causal an error is, **not** a frequency or difficulty measure — topped by missing the second table for a self-join (8 hypotheses), then `AVG` in `WHERE` instead of `HAVING`, joining all tables whose primary keys you need, not understanding when to apply `GROUP BY`, and not understanding subqueries (7 each) ([Miedema, Fletcher & Aivaloglou, TOCE](https://pure.tue.nl/ws/files/261867232/3551392.pdf)).

Three conclusions follow and they are sharp. First, **"which columns may appear in SELECT alongside an aggregate" is the single most error-dense micro-concept in all of SQL** — 0.40 persistent grouping-column errors in aggregate queries, plus 26–29% of `GROUP BY`/`HAVING` error codes — and it deserves multiple dedicated levels rather than one. Second, **self-join is a genuine conceptual boss fight**: 98% failure with only 38% syntax attribution, the `scoping_selfjoin` misconception at 14%, missing expression at 0.65, and the top expert hypothesis count, all converging on the same difficulty — aliasing one table as two logical roles. Third, the dominance of omission means the right intervention is structural, not more syntax drilling: visible requirement checklists, row-count previews that make a missing join's Cartesian blow-up *visible*, and a "did you use every table you mentioned?" check attack absence-of-retrieval-cue errors directly, whereas drills do not. Separately, the two highest-prevalence surface-syntax misconceptions (`is` 28%, `brackets` 11%, `neq` 6%) are cheap kills for a REPL with good error messages and belong in a tutorial tier, while `scoping_with` at 26% is a real conceptual gap that is high *precisely because* curricula barely teach CTEs.

Curricula genuinely disagree about the middle of the syllabus, which means a new game's ordering is a defensible opinion rather than a copy job. SQLBolt teaches clause by clause with `JOIN` at L6–7 **before** aggregates at L10–11, `NULL` at L8 immediately after `OUTER JOIN`, order-of-execution deferred to L12 as a retroactive explanation of confusion already experienced, DML/DDL at L13–18, a "SQL Review" consolidation checkpoint after L4 — and **no CTE lesson at all**, with subqueries and set operations pushed out of the numbered spine into optional "Additional Topics" ([SQLBolt](https://sqlbolt.com/)). pgexercises inverts the key axis across **7 categories and 81 exercises**: Simple SQL Queries, **JOINs and Subqueries**, Modifying Data, Aggregation, Working with Timestamps, String Operations, Recursive Queries ([pgexercises categories](https://docs.yugabyte.com/stable/develop/sample-data/pgexercises/)). SQLZoo is closer to the opposite of SQLBolt, putting nested `SELECT` before aggregation and both before `JOIN`, with `NULL` deferred until after all joins and self-join last ([SQLZoo study guide](https://github.com/chesterheng/sql-zoo)) — though that is a third-party guide, not the site's own index. Everyone agrees on the floor (`SELECT` + `WHERE`) and the ceiling (window functions, recursive CTEs, query plans); the contested middle is `{JOIN, aggregation, subquery, NULL}`. The error data resolves it on evidence rather than taste: grouping errors concentrate in `GROUP BY` work and column-resolution errors in join work, so **the two skills fail for different reasons, which argues for single-table aggregation first (cheap, isolated failure mode), then joins, then aggregation-over-joins as an explicitly taught third skill** — because that combination is exactly where the 0.31–0.40 grouping errors live. Nobody teaches `NULL` before joins, and SQLBolt's L7→L8 ordering (introduce `NULL` as a consequence of `OUTER JOIN`, after the learner has produced NULLs themselves) is the better-motivated of the two observed patterns. pgexercises' timestamps and string-operations categories are absent from every beginner curriculum and heavily represented in real analytics work — a strong candidate for a mid-game practical branch. Verified ordered lesson lists could not be obtained for Select Star SQL, DataLemur, StrataScratch, HackerRank, W3Schools, Khan Academy, DataCamp, or LeetCode SQL 50's official groupings, so claims of cross-platform "consensus" rest on SQLBolt, pgexercises and SQLZoo only.

On how to diagnose a wrong query, the strongest prior art is SQL-Tutor's constraint-based modelling, and its effectiveness number is the best available for any SQL teaching intervention found. It has **no domain module**: it checks the student's solution against domain knowledge encoded as **more than 500 constraints**, each a pair of a relevance condition and a satisfaction condition, diagnosed by pattern matching — relevance patterns first, then satisfaction patterns for the relevant ones — and **the student model is the set of violated constraints**. Four evaluation studies in 1998–2000 found significantly higher results for users, with the **1998 study, a single two-hour session, producing an improvement of nearly one standard deviation** ([Mitrovic et al., "Constraint-Based Tutors: a Success Story"](https://www.csse.canterbury.ac.nz/tanja.mitrovic/cbmtut.pdf)). One correction the spec should carry forward: **the frequently-cited six-level graded feedback scheme belongs to KERMIT, the database design tutor, not to SQL-Tutor** — anyone citing "SQL-Tutor's six feedback levels" is propagating a misattribution. The complementary approach is SQLab's fingerprinting, which hashes query results with a **40-bit hash** to distinguish far more error types than binary grading, exploiting logical clause evaluation order so that clauses at or before the `SELECT` list are fingerprinted in one pass while `DISTINCT`/`ORDER BY`/`LIMIT` need a second pass with a control value. In a worked example, a one-dimensional fingerprint formula detected **1 of 8 complications and 4 of 10 errors**; a two-dimensional formula detected **2 more complications and 3 more errors** — moving from a crude correctness check to a richer diagnostic roughly doubled detected error types. SQLab has been tested with roughly **300 students over three years** across two games, 30 exercises and a mock exam, and its Sessform database carries **over 500 feedback messages** covering 30 exercises, 10 mock-exam questions and a 36-episode adventure ([arXiv 2410.16120](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.16120)).

The two techniques are complementary, not competing: **constraints catch structural errors in the query text (missing join, aggregate in `WHERE`), fingerprinting catches behavioural divergence on crafted data (duplicate rows, NULL handling, ordering)**. A game attacking the persistent-error profile wants both, because the omission errors that dominate persistence are structural while the NULL and duplicate errors are behavioural. SQLab also names the constraint that most threatens naive grading: **result-set comparison on a single dataset will mark logically wrong queries correct** — a query can return correct rows on one instance while being logically wrong — so each level needs either multiple test datasets or a structural check, or the game will actively teach the misconceptions it is trying to cure. The sobering scope note is that 500+ constraints is hand-authored content; for a 60+ level game the realistic path is a small constraint set per level targeted at that level's known misconceptions, drawn from the prevalence tables above rather than from general-purpose SQL analysis.

On gamification itself the honest verdict is that the evidence is unavailable, not that it is positive. The relevant primary literature was located — an empirical gamification-and-SQL study in ACM TOCE ([Morales-Trujillo et al.](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3427597)), a **causality-targeted** badge study in SQL-Tutor ([RPTEL 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41039-022-00180-4)), SQL Scrolls as a reusable DGBL experiment ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3507923.3507932)), and a learning-from-errors gamification study ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3587102.3588821)) — but **paywalls and client errors blocked extraction of every quantitative result**. No effect sizes, sample sizes or significance values for any gamification study were obtained, and **no SQL-specific empirical evidence at all** was found on XP systems, streaks, leaderboards, spaced repetition, mastery learning, worked examples, or productive failure. The defensible position on what *was* extractable: **XP, badges and streaks are retention scaffolding at best, while diagnosis quality is the actual learning mechanism** — SQLab's doubling of detected error types and SQL-Tutor's ~1 SD both sit on the feedback side of that line.

## Progression works in two decoupled layers and hints should be priced in time

The best-regarded learning systems run two progression spines, not one. Khan Academy's is a directly implementable state machine: five states (Not Started, Attempted, Familiar, Proficient, Mastered), where Attempted means an exercise completed with **<70% correct**, Familiar **70–99%**, Proficient **100%**, and Mastered requires being already Proficient **and** answering the skill's questions correctly in a **mixed-skill assessment** — so **the top level cannot be reached by grinding one exercise**. Decay is only ever one step and only via that mixed assessment (Mastered→Proficient, Proficient→Familiar, Familiar→Attempted), and it is forgiving at question level: the Mastery Challenge is **6 questions covering 3 skills, 2 per skill**, and **one wrong of the two does not demote** — both must be wrong. The challenge unlocks at Familiar on ≥3 skills plus Proficient on ≥1 plus **>12 hours since the last one**, one per 12-hour window, resumable within 12 hours ([Khan Academy](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/360037494231); [mastery levels](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/bg/articles/5548760867853)). Codewars supplies the second spine and the anti-grind rules: ranks 8 kyu up to 1 kyu then 1–8 dan; points per kata roughly triple per tier (8 kyu = 2, 7 kyu = 3, 6 kyu = 8, 5 kyu = 21, 4 kyu = 55, 3 kyu = 149, 2 kyu = 404, 1 kyu = 1,097) against sharply rising thresholds (7 kyu = 20, 6 kyu = 76, 5 kyu = 229, 4 kyu = 643, 3 kyu = 1,768, 2 kyu = 4,829, 1 kyu = 13,147, 1 dan = 35,759, 2 dan = 97,225); **rank rises only on a kata not previously completed in any language, once per kata, and forfeited kata give zero progress**; and the relative-difficulty payoff curve structurally pushes players upward (+2 tiers ≈ +30% of a level, +1 ≈ +12%, equal ≈ +5%, −1 ≈ +1.7%, −2 ≈ +0.3%, −3 ≈ +0.09%). Honor and rank are decoupled on purpose, and the docs do **not** state that rank gates content ([Codewars docs](https://docs.codewars.com/gamification/ranks/)).

Exercism contributes the architecture that makes a 60–100 level game maintainable: **unlocking is computed, not hand-configured.** Concept exercises declare which concepts they teach, practice exercises declare prerequisite concepts, and a practice exercise unlocks once its prerequisites are covered — several concept exercises can unlock at once ([Exercism v3 rationale](https://github.com/exercism/v3/blob/master/docs/rationale-for-v3.md)). Concept exercises are "very tightly focused" with usually one expected approach; practice exercises are open-ended; the docs state the two are "fundamentally different" and that a good concept exercise is usually a bad practice exercise ([Exercism syllabus docs](https://exercism.org/docs/building/tracks/syllabus)). Their stated principles include "hand-waving allowed" — when two concepts are mutually dependent, simplify one and defer depth. And their post-mortem on why they rebuilt is directly relevant: v2's core/side model failed because maintainers **reordered exercises that were never designed to teach concepts**, producing a loop of attempt → be told the right way → redo that frustrated students and broke flow with long feedback waits. They also chose smaller, more constrained exercises because they are **easier to auto-analyze**, targeting ~95% automated analysis of iterations. (That rationale repo was archived 2021-08-31, so it is a historical plan document.)

Duolingo's 2022 redesign is the clearest natural experiment on gating, and its lesson is narrower than it looks. The branching tree was replaced by a linear path on **2022-11-01**, with skills and crowns removed, units made smaller and theme-grouped, guidebooks replacing skill tips, and Legendary rescoped from per-skill levels to whole-unit "legendary trophies" ([duoplanet](https://duoplanet.com/duolingo-new-learning-path-review/)). Duolingo says an **internal efficacy comparison found better reading and listening outcomes with the path than with the tree**, and the CEO's stated reasons were reducing confusion about when to advance versus review and keeping gamification from displacing the goal, targeting CEFR B2 ([itechguides](https://www.itechguides.com/duolingo-app-update-with-path-ui-facing-backlash-from-users-but-the-2026-controversy-is-bigger-than-the-redesign/)). The documented backlash, in rough order of prominence, was: loss of choice over which lesson to do; disorienting placement of long-term users; harder self-directed review because the system chooses; Stories harder to find; and an **XP penalty on review (5 XP for old content against a 10 XP minimum on the tree)** that disadvantaged path users in Leagues. **The backlash was overwhelmingly about loss of self-directed review, not about linearity per se** — which supports a strictly linear spine for early levels plus a persistent free-practice surface, rather than one or the other. A separate 2026 controversy is about course content, not the path: on **2026-04-01** Duolingo acknowledged learners hitting unfamiliar vocabulary on rebuilt courses, said it had not done enough to support the transition, said it was building catch-up lessons, and **paused adding users to the new courses** until those existed. Hearts-and-energy numbers could not be retrieved at all — no refill rate, cap or per-mistake cost — so that mechanic is unquantified here.

On difficulty shape, the usable heuristics are a sawtooth with rising peaks, where hard sections build tension and easy valleys **release tension, confirm competence and build anticipation**, plus stepped curves that hold a plateau after each jump so players master the new mechanic before the next. The specific ratios offered — **the first 20% of a game should cover only the first 30% of the difficulty range, the last 20% can cover the final 40%** — plus dynamic-difficulty triggers at **three consecutive failures** or **five clean sections**, with adjustments small enough (e.g. −10% enemy speed) that players still credit their own skill, come from a commercial game-dev content site rather than a primary source and should be treated as that author's heuristic ([abratabia](https://www.abratabia.com/game-design/difficulty-curves.php)). The Golden Idol team's empirical account is the honest counterweight: confusing scenarios got **more** clues, too-obvious ones got obfuscated, and clues were sometimes **removed** — in one case a written will was cut because it gave the answer away. They validated with **at least 5–7 playtests per scenario, watched in silence**, because knowing the answers made them unable to judge difficulty themselves; cost ran **one to two months per scenario**; and they admit they **never built a reliable framework** for designing mysteries ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol); [Thinky Games](https://thinkygames.com/features/how-the-case-of-the-golden-idol-developers-made-one-of-the-decades-best-detective-games-twice/)). They also identify a **"roof of complexity"** past which puzzles stop being fun, which scaling required new foundations to pass rather than more of the same.

The hint literature overturns the naive "hints are cheating, price them high" model. Gaming the system splits into two populations — **GAMED-HURT** (associated with poor learning) and **GAMED-NOT-HURT** (a different gaming style, *not* associated with poor learning) — distinguished by a detector at **A'=0.85 against non-gamers and A'=0.96 against GAMED-NOT-HURT students**. The top predictive features of the harmful signature are **requesting help on several consecutive actions on skills the student usually gets wrong** and **several very brief help requests in quick succession**. A cohort effect proves frequency and harm move independently: the 2004 cohort gamed ~88% more often than 2003 (t(175)=2.34, p=0.02) yet a *smaller* proportion of its gaming students showed poor learning (χ²(1,N=64)=6.01, p=0.01). Baker's explicit design warnings are the operative ones: a detector is useless without an intervention that changes behaviour, **if the system's response lets students learn to evade the detector it quickly becomes ineffective**, and systems should be built so that **appropriate use is the better choice for the student** ([Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Roll, 2005](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/BCKR2005Final.pdf)). The paper does not report an overall gaming percentage or a direct effect size for gaming on learning.

Against that, **time spent thinking around a hint predicts learning better than time-on-task does.** In the control condition (n=18), hint-related thinking time correlated with adjusted pre-post gain at **r=0.48 (p<0.05)**, post-hint reflection time at r=0.36 (p<0.10) and pre-answer thinking at r=0.34 (p<0.10), with hint-thinking time correlating with post-test at r=0.53 (p<0.05); in the experimental condition (n=19) hint-thinking was r=0.41 (p<0.05) and reflection r=0.35 (p<0.10). **By comparison time-on-task correlated with gain at only r=0.23, and average transaction time at r=0.20.** The authors' stated implication is that drilling to the bottom-out hint **should not automatically be treated as help abuse** — it may function as worked-example study driven by a self-explanation mechanism ([Shih, Koedinger & Scheines, 2008](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf)). Their caveats are substantial: samples of 18–19 (and 15 for a change analysis), correlational, off-task time assumed zero, and **no hint timing or delay manipulation was tested**. The combined implication is that the design lever is pacing and reflection, not price: make the harmful pattern (rapid-fire successive requests) expensive in *time*, and make the useful pattern (read, think, answer) free. Charging a currency is specifically contraindicated, because it makes the optimal play "hoard hints and guess," which is precisely the wrong frame — and note that the hint-costs-score mechanic appears independently in Lost at SQL and in DataCamp, where **requesting a hint or solution reduces or removes the XP for that task** ([DataCamp](https://support.datacamp.com/hc/en-us/articles/34043400793495-Understanding-XP-and-Progress-on-DataCamp)), with the obvious failure mode of penalising exactly the learner who most needs help and pushing them to the many published walkthroughs instead.

The lives-and-failure evidence is real but thin and points the opposite way to the usual anti-hearts argument. In a quasi-experimental case study of an educational programming game with **8 participants (3 experts, 5 novices)**, replacing instant-death-and-restart with **three visible lives** produced **100% completion with lives against 40% without — and all of those completers were experts**. Post-test scores showed little relationship to in-game behaviour, so the authors leaned on observed frustration and completion and label the retention claims **preliminary**. Their mechanism is that experts treat trial-and-error as a normal mechanic while **novices were simply discouraged**, and their recommendations are a small number of visible lives, difficulty matched to audience, **checkpoints** (non-completers named their absence as a major frustration), timely scaffolded feedback, and easier access to collected clues ([JAID 13(2)](https://edtechbooks.org/jaid_13_2/getting_to_the_3_of_things__how_game_feedback_enables_failure_for_learning)). The honest reading: this compares lives against instant death, **not lives against unlimited attempts**, so it supports graduated failure over total failure and says nothing in favour of a depleting access gate. **Nothing found in this research supports an access-gating depleting resource in a learning game**, and the clearest signal in the corpus is the recommendation to add checkpoints — the opposite of removing attempts. Chase et al. (2021, BJET), "Can typical game features have unintended consequences?", is the most on-point paper for the anti-hearts argument and **could not be read**; its findings must not be characterised.

Mystery structure converges on one pattern everywhere it appears. The **Three-Clue Rule** states: "For any conclusion you want the PCs to make, include at least three clues," justified by redundancy — miss the first, misread the second, and the third keeps the scenario on track. Its **inversion** is equally load-bearing: "If the PCs have access to ANY three clues, they will reach at least ONE conclusion." In the worked example the starting node holds three clues pointing to three different nodes; node A holds clues to B and C, so even after the clue that led to A is consumed the party still has leads; by node C they have accumulated **seven clues**. Critically, the resource accounting removes the authoring-budget excuse for a single chain: **a plotted linear version of the same scenario needs the same four nodes and nine clues**, while the node version is richer and leaves players in the driver's seat ([The Alexandrian, node-based design pt. 3](https://thealexandrian.net/?p=7985); [pt. 2](https://thealexandrian.net/creations/misc/node-design/node-design2.html)). The author stresses the rule concerns clue *access*, not guaranteed discovery or correct interpretation. Golden Idol converged independently — "where possible, multiple clue paths lead to the same conclusion" — and adds a "thought path" chaining scheme (a key identifies a door → a room → its occupant → the murderer), deliberately minimal evidence where items exist **only if needed for the solution or intended to misdirect**, and an "aha" goal defined explicitly as a shift from confusion to understanding.

Two different solutions to the brute-force problem are both portable. Obra Dinn **never confirms a single guess**: each fate has three parts (victim identity, mode of death, killer) entered from multiple-choice lists, and verification happens in **groups of three** — once three entered fates are correct, play pauses and they are permanently recorded in the ledger with a musical cue — because checking one at a time would let players click through combinations ([Film Stories](https://filmstories.co.uk/features/exploring-return-of-the-obra-dinns-rule-of-three/)). Its documented weakness is instructive: because *any* three can verify together, a confirmation also tells you every unverified entry is wrong, which patient players exploit to cycle options; the multiple-choice lists themselves leak thematic hints; and blind guessing still happens in most playthroughs, so the buffer "only partly works" ([Kokutech](https://www.kokutech.com/blog/gamedev/design-patterns/unique-mechanics/return-of-the-obra-dinn)). Golden Idol's answer is the opposite direction: judging a whole puzzle as simply right or wrong **frustrated players**, so they shipped an indicator reading **"two or fewer slots are incorrect"** — rewarding closeness without validating any individual slot — and added sub-puzzles (identifying people in the scene, plus one custom puzzle per scenario) specifically to supply steady progress inside a long case ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/case-of-the-golden-idol)). Obra Dinn's crew manifest, meanwhile, is both the progress record and the deduction instrument — **elimination on the manifest is an explicit deduction method** — and each solved identity unlocks memories, which is also the game's entire retention loop with zero social features. Her Story shows the opposite extreme and its own exploit: a search engine over **200+ interrogation clips** returning **only the first 5 videos** per search, with no notebook and no win state, where generic words like "she" unlock key clips very early and the cited analysis's fix is to restrict valid search terms ([Critical analysis of Her Story](https://danqiao.myportfolio.com/critical-analysis-of-her-story)).

Three more patterns are worth naming for the spec. **Case-as-clue**: Golden Idol set a high-level story archetype early (protagonist, antagonists, ending) but did not plan scenarios in detail, developing them non-chronologically; in the sequel, chapter-spanning mysteries frame each set and **each individual case becomes a clue in the larger mystery**, with the device being that vignettes look unrelated until **identical bracelets link them** — and solving any individual scenario does *not* require knowing what the bracelets mean ([Thinky Games](https://thinkygames.com/features/how-the-case-of-the-golden-idol-developers-made-one-of-the-decades-best-detective-games-twice/)). **Authored wrong answers**: Sherlock Holmes: Crimes & Punishments runs **six cases of up to ~three hours**, with clues entering a case book and a deduction board where some nodes offer more than one choice, so "you can solve the case how you want to solve it" and a case can end more than one way, followed by a moral decision tied to reputation ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2014/09/30/sherlock-holmes-crimes-punishments-review/)) — and walkthrough sites maintain per-case pages enumerating the game's **"other deductions (wrong)"**, confirming wrong deductions are authored content rather than a fail state ([gamepressure](https://www.gamepressure.com/sherlock/other-deductions-wrong/z168a2)). **Rules-as-mechanic**: Papers, Please delivers each new rule via a **daily bulletin**, and Lucas Pope specifically avoided posting rules on mornings when another character was already talking, because testers ignored the bulletin in those moments and got confused; he laid the schedule out by hand and warns "the entire game teeters on this complicated network of dependencies," running validation procedures that **generated thousands of immigrants** to test the random system ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-bleak-genius-of-i-papers-please-i-)). Disco Elysium adds the long-horizon optional-depth model — **up to 12 thoughts internalized at a time, 3 free slots with each additional slot costing one skill point, 53 thoughts listed**, a two-stage Research→Completion delayed payoff whose outcomes **are not always upgrades**, and the **white vs. red check distinction** where white checks reopen when conditions change while red checks are one-shot narrative forks ([itechguides](https://www.itechguides.com/how-disco-elysiums-thought-cabinet-and-skill-checks-work/)); those figures are version-specific by the source's own admission, and the source is a guide site rather than a designer statement.

Several named items in this area are **uncited and must not be presented as sourced**: Brilliant.org's structure (not researched), Advent of Code's two-part-per-day pattern, Baba Is You and The Witness comprehension-gating, Zachtronics' global optimization histogram (the interview reached covers the three metrics — **cost, area, cycles** — and leaderboards, and confirms there are **deliberately no in-game rewards for optimization**, which the designers say self-selects intrinsically motivated players ([Game Developer / IGF](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/road-to-the-igf-zachtronics-i-opus-magnum-i-))), Duolingo's pre-2022 checkpoints, Khan's mastery-points formula, Lucas Pope's own stated intent for batch-of-three, and Papers, Please's 31-day structure and citation economy. There is also **no research at all** on retention mechanics for account-free offline learning games, no source on collectibles in educational contexts, and no source on local-storage-loss mitigation in shipped browser games — everything in that area is extrapolation from single-player design.

## PGlite ships real PostgreSQL 18.6 to the browser for ~5 MB gzipped

The engine question is settled by measurement. **PGlite 0.5.13, published 2026-10-28, Apache-2.0, with zero runtime npm dependencies**, embeds real **PostgreSQL 18.6** — `select version()` on a live instance returns `PostgreSQL 18.6 (PGlite 0.5.13) on wasm32-unknown-emscripten`, confirmed independently by string-scanning both `pglite.data` and `pglite.wasm` ([npm registry](https://registry.npmjs.org/@electric-sql/pglite)). **Many secondary write-ups say PGlite embeds PG 16 or 17; as of 0.5.13 that is out of date.** All 36 SQL features tested against it passed: CTEs and `WITH RECURSIVE`, window functions, `generate_series` in integer and date/interval forms, `DISTINCT ON`, aggregate `FILTER (WHERE ...)`, `LATERAL`, JSONB including `jsonb_path_query`, full-text search via `to_tsvector`/`to_tsquery`/`@@`/`ts_rank` with **no extension needed**, `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS, FORMAT JSON)`, `GROUPING SETS`, `ROLLUP`, `FULL OUTER JOIN`, range types, `percentile_cont WITHIN GROUP`, set operations, `information_schema`/`pg_catalog`, `MERGE`, upsert with `ON CONFLICT ... RETURNING`, materialized views, generated and identity columns, declarative partitioning, PL/pgSQL functions with row triggers, savepoints, `COPY`, GIN and GiST indexes, roles and grants, sequences, enums and domains, and `gen_random_uuid()` without pgcrypto. **For a teaching game PGlite is not an approximation of Postgres; it is Postgres** — and working `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, FORMAT JSON)` enables query-plan teaching levels no SQLite-based competitor can match. Warm performance is ample: seeding **10,000 rows across 2 tables with an index and `ANALYZE` took 338 ms**, and a join + `GROUP BY` + window-function analytic query over it took **11 ms**.

The cost is payload and cold start, and the numbers matter for the spec. Measured raw and gzip -9: `pglite.wasm` **9,087,748 → 3,246,869 bytes (3.10 MB)**, `pglite.data` **6,248,509 → 1,865,924 (1.78 MB)**, plus `pglite.js` glue at 426,292 and the main client chunk at 375,416 — **a critical path of roughly 5.1–5.3 MB gzipped, about 16 MB raw**. The project's own claim of "under 3mb Gzipped" ([pglite.dev/docs/about](https://pglite.dev/docs/about)) conflicts with that measurement; the WASM alone gzips to 3.10 MB, so the ~3 MB figure appears to count only the WASM module or reflect an older PG 16-era build. **Trust the measured ~5 MB.** Brotli was unavailable in the measuring container, so ~5 MB gzip is likely a pessimistic upper bound for Brotli-serving hosts. On cold start, the decisive finding is that **restoring a pre-built `dumpDataDir` snapshot takes 762/748/716 ms, roughly 2.5x faster than running `initdb` fresh at 1,775–3,269 ms** (3,133 ms on a first-ever cold start). Compilation is not the bottleneck — `WebAssembly.compile(pglite.wasm)` alone is **40 ms** — and passing a precompiled module via `pgliteWasmModule` + `fsBundle` still took 2,184 ms, because the cost is `initdb` and catalog bootstrap. **Every timing here was measured in Node.js on Linux, not in a browser; real browser cold start will differ and will be worse on mobile. Browser-measured cold start is the single most important number still missing.**

Three facts make this work on a free static host. First, **PGlite needs no cross-origin isolation**: grepping every JS file in `dist/`, `dist/worker/` and `dist/fs/` returned **zero occurrences of `SharedArrayBuffer`, `Atomics.wait`, `crossOriginIsolated` or `USE_PTHREADS`**, which the filesystems docs corroborate — so it runs on GitHub Pages, where response headers cannot be set. That is the single biggest differentiator against DuckDB-WASM, which ships a distinct `duckdb-coi.wasm` plus pthread workers for its parallel build. Second, bare-CDN loading works with no bundler because the Emscripten glue resolves its binaries relative to its own module URL — exactly `new URL("./pglite.wasm", import.meta.url)`, `new URL("./pglite.data", import.meta.url)` and `new URL("./initdb.wasm", import.meta.url)` appear in the dist — though `dist/index.js` is a 479-byte re-export shim pointing at sibling chunks, so the whole graph must be served from one directory. Third, an **official worker wrapper ships in the package**: `worker()` inside the worker file and `PGliteWorker` on the main thread, with leader election that nominates one tab to run the instance and re-elects when it closes, exposing `onLeaderChange` and `isLeader` ([pglite.dev/docs/multi-tab-worker](https://pglite.dev/docs/multi-tab-worker)). Because **PGlite is single user / single connection** — Emscripten programs cannot fork, so it runs Postgres in single-user mode with a custom I/O pathway — the leader pattern is not polish, it is how you stop two tabs corrupting one `idb://` directory.

Persistence has one clear answer and one clear trap. Of the schemes confirmed both in docs and by grepping the dist for literal prefixes (`memory://`, `idb://`, `opfs-ahp://`, `file://`), **the docs explicitly recommend IndexedDB for browser use "for now," because Safari does not support OPFS** ([pglite.dev/docs/filesystems](https://pglite.dev/docs/filesystems)). OPFS AHP requires a Web Worker, works on Chrome and Firefox only, and fails on Safari because Safari appears to cap open sync access handles at **252 while a standard Postgres install has over 300 files**. `idb://` is a layer over the in-memory FS — all DB files load into memory at start and flush to IndexedDB after each changing query, with each flush taking "a few milliseconds" — which means **memory use scales with total DB size regardless of query size**, a real constraint on iOS. `relaxedDurability` returns results immediately and schedules the flush asynchronously, and the docs **explicitly do not say** whether data can be lost if the page closes first, so treat it as "may lose the last query" and flush at explicit checkpoints. The VFSs are documented as "under active development and may change," the README still carries a **`status - alpha`** badge, and a release landed two days before this research — so **pin exact versions and vendor the files rather than floating a CDN tag**. Extensions are opt-in: a default instance returned only **2 rows from `pg_available_extensions` (`pg_trgm`, `plpgsql`)**, and `pg_trgm` only because it was explicitly passed, so a bare instance effectively offers plpgsql alone. **37 extension tarballs ship inside `dist/`** (amcheck through uuid-ossp) and still need registering; `pgcrypto.tar.gz` is a **1.1 MB** outlier to avoid loading casually; pgvector (42.9 KB) and PostGIS (**18.8 MB**) are separate packages entirely ([pglite.dev/extensions](https://pglite.dev/extensions/)). A bundled contrib was confirmed working end to end: importing `pg_trgm`, creating the extension, then `similarity('postgres','postgre')` → **0.7**. On memory, PGlite is wasm32, where **4 GB is the theoretical ceiling and Emscripten defaults to 2 GB unless built with `MAXIMUM_MEMORY=4GB`**, with V8 giving no figure for what is reliably allocatable and noting many machines lack that much free memory ([v8.dev](https://v8.dev/blog/4gb-wasm-memory)).

Snapshots are the reset mechanism and they are not free. `dumpDataDir(compression?)` returns a gzipped tarball, loaded back through the constructor's `loadDataDir` option (there is no separate load method), and the docs warn the dump is **only designed for re-import into PGlite**. Measured: an empty-ish DB dumped to **4,611,973 bytes in 665 ms** gzipped (40,760,832 bytes in 114 ms uncompressed), and a 10k-row DB to **5,184,544 bytes in 731 ms**. **So a shippable pre-built snapshot of a small teaching database is ~5 MB gzipped — the same order as the WASM payload, roughly doubling first-load bytes if you ship one.** The implied strategy is therefore a hybrid: ship **one** base snapshot shared across levels and apply small per-level `.sql` deltas via `.exec()` (which runs multiple statements through the simple protocol), since 338 ms for 10k rows makes deltas nearly free. Shipping a snapshot also lets you drop `initdb.wasm` (300,109 bytes) and `initdb.js` (105,955) from the critical path.

No alternative engine is better for this job and the gap is not close. Measured gzip -9 sizes: **sql.js 1.15.2 (MIT) at 334,992 bytes** for `sql-wasm.wasm`, the official **`@sqlite.org/sqlite-wasm` 3.54.1-build1 (Apache-2.0) at 406,471**, and **DuckDB-WASM's `duckdb-eh.wasm` at 7,034,942 — 2.3x PGlite's WASM** (with `duckdb-mvp.wasm` at 7,958,692), whose npm `latest` tag is a **`-dev` prerelease (1.33.1-dev57.0, last published 2026-06-22)**. `wa-sqlite` **declares no license** and was last published 2024-01-05, ~2.8 years stale. **DuckDB-WASM is not a smaller fallback; it is strictly worse here** — larger, needing COOP/COEP for its parallel build, and still not Postgres, with its only genuine edge being analytical performance on large data that a teaching game does not need. The real choice is binary: **real Postgres at ~5 MB, or ~400 KB SQLite with a dialect compromise.** That compromise is large enough to matter: `DISTINCT ON`, `LATERAL`, `GROUPING SETS`/`ROLLUP`, `MERGE`, range types, `percentile_cont WITHIN GROUP`, date-form `generate_series`, true JSONB with `jsonb_path_query`, `to_tsvector`/`@@`/`ts_rank`, `GENERATED ... AS IDENTITY`, declarative partitioning, PL/pgSQL, `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, FORMAT JSON)` and `information_schema` all worked in PGlite and are absent, differently spelled or non-idiomatic in SQLite, which also has dynamic typing and no native date type. There is a pedagogical argument too, not just a feature count: **Postgres's strict typing surfaces the data-type-mismatch errors that appear in Taipalus' persistent-error data (0.07 in subqueries, 0.04 in self-joins) where SQLite silently coerces them away**, and the richest error-frequency dataset in the literature is itself a set of **Postgres error codes** (42803, 42703, 42702), so a Postgres-backed game can map real engine errors onto a research-validated taxonomy directly.

For analysis rather than execution, **libpg-query 18.2.2 (MIT, published 2026-10-29) is the standout: `libpg-query.wasm` gzips to 338,351 bytes plus ~21 KB of JS**, and its version number tracks Postgres 18, matching PGlite's 18.6 engine. It is Postgres's real `gram.y` parser compiled to WASM, so its AST is authoritative parse output — ideal for "did the student use a JOIN, a subquery, a window function?" checks and for rejecting syntax before execution, at **7% of PGlite's size**. `pgsql-parser` 18.3.1 is a thin wrapper over it (12,703 bytes unpacked). The budget alternative is `node-sql-parser` 5.4.0's per-dialect UMD build: **`umd/postgresql.umd.js` at 66,451 bytes gzipped** instead of the 427,807-byte all-dialect bundle, loadable from a plain `<script>` with no bundler, at the cost of being a hand-written grammar rather than Postgres's own. **Avoid `sql-parser-cst` 0.43.0 — it is GPL-2.0-or-later**, a copyleft licence that may be unacceptable for a distributed game. The recommended architecture is to use both: PGlite in a worker to execute, libpg-query to analyse, which gives instant offline pre-execution structural feedback. Note that DuckDB-WASM, sql.js and libpg-query were **not executed** in this research — only PGlite's feature matrix is execution-verified — and libpg-query's usability from a plain `<script type="module">` is unverified.

On storage and durability, quota is a non-issue and eviction is the real threat. Per-origin limits for IndexedDB, Cache API and OPFS (one shared system) run to **~60% of total disk on Chrome/Chromium/Edge** (with Chrome using at most 80% browser-wide), **the smaller of 10% of disk or 10 GiB on Firefox** as a group limit (rising to 50% capped at 8 TiB when persistent and exempt from the group limit), and **~60% on Safari browser, ~15% in embedded WebViews, ~1/10 of the parent's quota for cross-origin frames**; quotas are computed from total disk size, not free space ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria)). Web Storage is the one to watch: MDN says both "limited to 10 MiB on all browsers" and "up to 5 MiB of local storage and 5 MiB of session storage per origin," and itself advises treating **5 MiB per origin per storage type** as the practical figure, with `QuotaExceededError` on exceeding it. Default storage is **best-effort and can be evicted without interrupting the user**; `navigator.storage.persist()` upgrades it to persistent, which only the user can delete — **Firefox shows a prompt, while Safari and Chromium auto-approve or deny silently based on interaction history**, so the call should come after a real user gesture rather than on page load. Eviction has three triggers: storage pressure (LRU by origin, skipping persistent ones), browser maximum exceeded, and — **the single biggest progress-loss risk — Safari's proactive rule, where with cross-site tracking prevention on, an origin with no user interaction in the last seven days of browser use has its script-created data deleted.** When eviction fires, **all of an origin's data goes together**, IndexedDB and Cache alike. Crucially, **whether `persist()` exempts an origin from Safari's 7-day ITP deletion is not stated by MDN**, which describes ITP as a separate eviction rule — treat "persist() protects you on Safari" as **unverified and probably false**.

On the surrounding stack, the editor decision is also settled by measurement. **CodeMirror 6 costs ~274,891 bytes gzipped across the nine required packages** — `@codemirror/view` 113,456, `state` 34,294, `language` 26,560, `autocomplete` 22,143, `@lezer/common` 19,921, `@lezer/lr` 17,469, `commands` 16,967, `lang-sql` 16,102, `@lezer/highlight` 7,979 — which is **~5.4% of PGlite's payload**, so arguing about it once a 5 MB engine is accepted is misallocated effort, especially since autocomplete seeded from the level's schema is a teaching feature. **Monaco (101,674,083 bytes unpacked across 1,918 files) and Ace (58,597,099 bytes across 2,175 files) are disqualified** for a no-bundler static site ([npm monaco-editor](https://registry.npmjs.org/monaco-editor); [npm ace-builds](https://registry.npmjs.org/ace-builds)). A textarea with a Prism core + `prism-sql` highlight overlay is the near-zero option and a plain `<textarea>` at 0 KB is a legitimate v1. The overall budget is **~5.1 MB PGlite + ~275 KB CodeMirror + ~338 KB libpg-query ≈ 5.7 MB gzipped first load, near zero on repeat visits behind a service worker** — the engine dominates and everything else is noise. **Everything in this paragraph beyond the measured sizes, and all of the service-worker, MIME-type, caching, routing, state-management and save-versioning guidance in the notes, is uncited engineering judgement rather than sourced fact**, including the recommendations to serve PGlite from your own origin under a version-pinned path, precompress to `.br`/`.gz`, serve `.wasm` as `application/wasm`, use hash routing on static hosts, and keep progress records out of Postgres entirely. CodeMirror 6 loading from `esm.sh` with no bundler was not verified, and no mobile or touch testing of any editor option was done.

Finally, accessibility sets hard constraints that shape the deduction UI before a line is written. WCAG 2.2's **2.5.7 Dragging Movements (Level AA)** makes a drag-only evidence board non-conformant, so the board must be a selection model with drag layered on top. **1.4.1 Use of Color (Level A)** forbids signalling correctness by colour alone — which the natural SQL encoding satisfies for free, since a text verdict line like "runs; 3 of 5 rows match" is also the partial-correctness mechanic. **2.2.1 Timing Adjustable (Level A)** requires every time limit to be disableable, extendable to at least ten times the default, or extendable after a warning with at least 20 seconds to respond, which effectively **forbids timers on the critical path** and pushes all timing into an opt-in efficiency layer. Also in scope: **2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (AA)**, **2.4.13 Focus Appearance (AAA)** requiring a focus indicator at least the area of a **2 CSS pixel thick perimeter** with **≥3:1 contrast** between states, **1.4.3 Contrast** at **≥4.5:1** (≥3:1 for large text), **1.4.12 Text Spacing** surviving line height 1.5×, paragraph spacing 2×, letter spacing 0.12× and word spacing 0.16×, and **2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA)** ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/2023/REC-WCAG22-20231005/)). **The exact normative text and pixel value for 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum) could not be extracted** — the widely-cited figure is 24×24 CSS pixels but it was not verified in the spec and should be looked up. No game-accessibility-specific source was reachable, and no source was found on screen-reader handling of code editors or SQL result grids, which is a real under-documented problem for this genre. For English plus Bahasa Indonesia, a documented framework-free client-side i18n pattern exists for this exact stack ([Intlayer vanilla JS docs](https://intlayer.org/id/doc/environment/vanilla)), cited only as evidence the pattern exists rather than as a recommendation; the commonly quoted ~20–30% text-expansion allowance is an industry rule of thumb and **is not sourced here**.

## Design implications: what a new SQL detective game must do to beat the incumbents

The following is ordered by leverage — what most changes the odds of beating SQL Murder Mystery and SQL Noir, given the evidence above. Items 1–4 are the thesis; 5–9 are the execution; 10–12 are the constraints that will sink the project if ignored.

**1. Grade the query, not the name. This is the whole differentiation thesis.** Both incumbents compare an answer string; the only project that tried query-level feedback is not public. Build a two-layer diagnosis: **libpg-query (338 KB gzipped, Postgres 18's own parser) for structural checks on the AST before execution**, and **PGlite execution plus SQLab-style result fingerprinting for behavioural checks**. They are complementary — constraints catch the omission errors that dominate persistence (missing join, missing expression, aggregate in `WHERE`), fingerprinting catches duplicate-row, NULL and ordering divergence. Author per-level constraint pairs on SQL-Tutor's relevance/satisfaction model and treat **the set of violated constraints as the player's skill model**, which is exactly the input a mastery gate needs. Do not attempt general-purpose SQL analysis: SQL-Tutor needed **500+ hand-authored constraints**, so scope to a handful per level targeted at that level's known misconceptions. And heed SQLab's warning that **result-set comparison on a single dataset marks logically wrong queries correct** — every level needs either multiple seed datasets or a structural check, or the game will teach the misconceptions it exists to cure.

**2. Make the curriculum attack the measured error profile, not the clause list.** Spend level budget in proportion to where learners actually fail persistently. **"Which columns may appear in SELECT alongside an aggregate" earns multiple dedicated levels** (0.40 persistent grouping-column errors in aggregate queries; 26–29% of `GROUP BY`/`HAVING` error codes). **Self-join earns its own chapter as a conceptual boss fight** (98% failure, only 38% syntax, `scoping_selfjoin` at 14%, top expert hypothesis count) framed explicitly as aliasing one table into two logical roles. **CTEs earn mid-curriculum treatment as a decomposition and readability tool**, not just as a vehicle for `RECURSIVE` at the end — SQLBolt has no CTE lesson at all, pgexercises reaches them only in its final category, and `scoping_with` sits at 26% prevalence precisely because of that gap. Sequence the contested middle on evidence: **single-table aggregation first, then joins, then aggregation-over-joins as an explicitly separate third skill**, since the two fail for different reasons. Introduce `NULL` as a consequence of `OUTER JOIN` (SQLBolt's L7→L8), not as abstract three-valued logic. Add pgexercises' **timestamps and string-operations** branch, which no beginner curriculum has and real analytics work demands. Kill the cheap surface misconceptions (`is` 28%, `brackets` 11%, `neq` 6%) in a tutorial tier with good error messages rather than dedicating design to them. Copy SQLBolt's two structural devices: a **consolidation checkpoint** after the first clause cluster, and a **mental-model reveal** (execution order) placed after all clauses have been met, as a retroactive explanation of confusion already experienced.

**3. Fix error messages first, because that is where learners quit.** **54% of executions are syntax errors and 51% of unsuccessful students abandon after failing to fix one.** Every game in the field passes the engine's error string straight through, which is why SQL Island's own students asked for better error messages while completing at 91%. Intercept the Postgres error codes and rewrite them pedagogically — 42601 syntax error, 42703 undefined column, 42803 grouping error, 42P01 undefined table, 42702 ambiguous column are **47% of all errors between them** and map cleanly onto a research-validated taxonomy because the engine is Postgres. Specifically cover the hole an educator found in SMM's walkthrough: warn about identically-named columns on joins *before* the learner hits the ambiguous-column error. Add structural scaffolds aimed at omission: a visible requirement checklist, a **row-count preview that makes a missing join's Cartesian blow-up visible**, and a "did you use every table you mentioned?" check.

**4. Build the progression as two decoupled layers, and compute unlocks from a DAG.** Layer 1 is **per-concept mastery on Khan's five-state machine** (Not Started / Attempted / Familiar / Proficient / Mastered) with **one-step-only, asymmetric decay reachable only through a mixed-skill assessment the learner chose to start** — never below Attempted, and never from grinding one exercise. Layer 2 is a **Detective Rank that, Codewars-style, advances only on first completion and gives zero credit for replays or revealed answers**, and which must be **re-derivable from the per-level completion set** rather than stored as an accumulated integer, because a cleared browser would otherwise wipe both layers at once. Adopt **Exercism's computed unlocking**: each level declares `teaches: [concepts]` and `requires: [concepts]`, and availability is computed, not hand-authored — this is the single highest-leverage architectural choice for a 60–100 level game because it makes reordering and insertion cheap, and it needs a **build-time DAG validator** to catch cycles and over-specified prerequisites that silently lock a subtree. Gate top-tier mastery behind Khan's Mastery Challenge shape (**3 concepts, 2 queries each**, drawn randomly from a large per-concept pool so repeats are not the same six questions), replacing the 12-hour cooldown with a **work gate** — N new filed evidence items from distinct concepts — because a static client's clock cannot be trusted. Keep the efficiency/optimization layer entirely off the progression path: Zachtronics offers **no in-game reward for optimization** and Codewars decouples honor from rank, so par badges and histogram positions should feed status and never unblock the next level.

**5. Run a linear spine early, then open up — and ship a free-practice surface from the start.** Duolingo's path measurably improved beginner outcomes by the company's own account, and the backlash was overwhelmingly about **loss of self-directed review**, not linearity. So: strict linear lock-step for roughly the first 15 levels, then hub-and-spoke branching, plus a persistent archive ("Cold Case Files") where the learner chooses what to drill — **gated to show only concepts already at ≥Attempted**, so beginners cannot wander into untaught material. Gate the hub exit on a **mastery threshold, not a completion count**, or learners cherry-pick the easiest spoke and arrive under-prepared. Shape difficulty as a sawtooth with a deliberate rest level after every spike; if 80 levels, the first ~16 cover only the easiest ~30% of the range and the last ~16 the hardest ~40% (treating those ratios as a heuristic, not a finding). Tag every level with an authored difficulty 1–10 and a `role` of `teach | drill | spike | rest | mastery`, then **lint the sequence at build time** to assert no spike follows a spike and every spike gets a rest within two levels. Expect authored difficulty estimates to be systematically too low — the Golden Idol team reported exactly that about themselves — so budget **5–7 silent playtests per case**.

**6. Price hints in time and reflection, never in currency; make the bottom rung a worked example.** The harmful gaming signature is **several very brief help requests in quick succession** on skills the learner usually gets wrong; the productive pattern is read-hint-think-answer, where hint-thinking time predicts learning at **r=0.48 against time-on-task's r=0.23**. So build a four-rung ladder (nudge → conceptual hint → worked step → answer) where each rung past the first requires either a **short enforced dwell of a few seconds** or an interstitial micro-commitment ("which clause do you need next?"). The dwell must be visibly framed as re-reading the evidence rather than punishment, must never apply to the first rung, and must be skippable once the learner has already failed the level. **Charge no currency and deduct no score** — that makes the optimal play "hoard hints and guess," and penalises exactly the learner who needs help, which is how Lost at SQL and DataCamp push stuck learners to published walkthroughs. **Make the answer rung a query annotated clause by clause, then require the learner to type a variant that differs in one parameter**, so transcription alone fails; that single mechanic converts bottom-out use from abuse into the worked-example study the evidence supports. Instrument the harmful signature with an honest hand-built heuristic (≥3 consecutive bottom-out hints on concepts previously failed) and respond with **content — a skippable remedial sequence on the prerequisite — never by locking hints**, since Baker's warning is that a response learners can evade becomes useless.

**7. Replace binary verdicts with a graded diagnosis ladder, and make failure narrative.** Judging a whole puzzle right or wrong frustrated Golden Idol's players; their fix was **"two or fewer slots are incorrect"** — closeness without locating the error. The SQL-native ladder is: parse error → runs but wrong shape → right shape wrong rows → right rows wrong order → correct, reported as counts and shapes ("runs; returns the right columns; row count is off"), **capping detail so deduction does not become mechanical patching**. Then go further than any incumbent: **author the signature wrong answer as content.** A learner who writes an inner join where a left join was needed should accuse the wrong suspect *because the join silently dropped the rows that exonerate them*, and then get a chance to revisit. This is the most pedagogically valuable mechanic in the detective corpus because SQL's characteristic bugs return confident wrong answers, and Crimes & Punishments proves a mystery game can let the player be confidently wrong and continue — walkthrough sites enumerate its "other deductions (wrong)" as content. It roughly doubles authoring cost per level, so **apply it only on known-trap concepts: joins, NULL semantics, GROUP BY, aggregate filtering**. Add checkpoints everywhere and make nearly every level a Disco-Elysium "white check" that reopens — and reopens *differently* — once the learner has the missing concept, reserving irreversibility for case verdicts.

**8. Architect cases as node graphs on the Three-Clue Rule, with an answer layer that punishes cycling.** Every conclusion a case requires should be reachable by **three independent query paths**, and the Alexandrian's resource accounting removes the excuse — a branching version of a scenario costs the same four nodes and nine clues as a linear one. Validate at build time for (i) ≥3 inbound clue paths per load-bearing conclusion and (ii) **reachability with each single clue removed**, not merely clue counts, because authors drift toward one intended path and let the other two decay into decoration. For the case verdict use **Obra Dinn's batch verification — confirm only when three independent assertions are simultaneously right** — but learn from its documented leak: a confirmation tells you every unconfirmed entry is wrong, so the accusation sheet needs more than three slots and must confirm in threes **without naming which three**. The deepest anti-brute-force lever, though, is data design, not UI: avoid single-scalar answers a guess can land, and **randomize seed data per playthrough within schema-preserving bounds, with a visible seed the learner can quote**, so the shareable answer is a query rather than a value — which also solves googleability, since a walkthrough can then only publish the reasoning, which is the hint ladder's own worked-example rung. Make the notebook mechanical rather than decorative: an auto-accreting case file tagged with the query that produced each result set, searchable and usable as input to later levels — but **require the learner to mark which rows matter** before they count as filed evidence, or the notebook does the remembering and kills retrieval practice.

**9. Use case-as-clue for the metaplot, and a diegetic bulletin for new rules.** Each case stands alone and is solvable without the metaplot; a recurring initially-meaningless motif (Golden Idol's identical bracelets) appears in every case's data as an anomalous row, column or account id that makes no sense locally; the motif's meaning *is* the metaplot, resolved in a late-game level that queries the accumulated case file. Golden Idol's guard is essential — **solving a scenario must never require knowing what the motif means**, or players crack the metaplot in case 2 and the rest is filler. Deliver each new SQL clause as a Papers, Please-style diegetic bulletin, and heed Pope's two concrete lessons: **never introduce a rule while something else competes for attention** (testers simply did not read it), and a hand-maintained dependency network "teeters," so **generate and validate** — the equivalent of Pope's thousands-of-immigrants validator is a harness that runs every level's reference solution against every randomized seed and asserts both that the reference passes and that a library of known-wrong queries fails. Set the high-level story arc early but author cases non-chronologically, and accept Golden Idol's cost signal: **one to two months per scenario**, with a "roof of complexity" past which puzzles stop working.

**10. Ship PGlite 0.5.13 in a Web Worker on `idb://`, with a shared base snapshot and per-level SQL deltas.** Pin the exact version, **vendor `dist/` into a version-pinned path on your own origin** rather than floating a CDN tag — the README still says `status - alpha`, the VFSs are documented as subject to change, and a release landed two days before this research. Budget **~5.1–5.3 MB gzipped** for the engine (measured 3.10 MB `pglite.wasm` + 1.78 MB `pglite.data` + ~0.4 MB glue), not the project's claimed "under 3mb," and treat Brotli as upside. Load via the **official `PGliteWorker` wrapper with leader election**, which is mandatory rather than optional because PGlite is single-connection and two tabs would otherwise corrupt one `idb://` directory. Use **`idb://`, not `opfs-ahp://`** — OPFS buys performance only on Chrome and Firefox and breaks Safari entirely over its 252 sync-handle cap against Postgres's 300+ files, and the docs themselves recommend IndexedDB for browsers for now. Reset levels by **restoring a pre-built snapshot (762/748/716 ms measured) rather than running `initdb` (1,775–3,269 ms)**, which also lets you drop `initdb.wasm` + `initdb.js` (~406 KB) from the critical path — but because a snapshot of even a small teaching DB is **~5 MB gzipped**, ship **one** base snapshot and apply per-level `.sql` deltas with `.exec()`, since seeding 10k rows takes 338 ms. Treat `relaxedDurability` as "may lose the last query" and flush explicitly at checkpoints. Load extensions deliberately — a bare instance offers plpgsql only, and `pgcrypto` alone is 1.1 MB. Add **libpg-query 18.2.2 (338 KB gzipped, matching Postgres 18)** for structural analysis, with `node-sql-parser`'s PostgreSQL-only UMD at 66 KB as the budget fallback, and **avoid `sql-parser-cst` (GPL-2.0-or-later)** and `wa-sqlite` (no declared license, stale since Jan 2024). **Do not treat DuckDB-WASM as a fallback** — at 7.03 MB gzipped it is 2.3x PGlite's WASM, wants COOP/COEP for its parallel build, and still is not Postgres. Use CodeMirror 6 (~275 KB gzipped across nine packages, ~5.4% of the engine) with schema-seeded autocomplete, and ship a plain textarea as v1 if that buys shipping sooner. **The one critical unknown here is browser cold start** — every timing above was measured in Node on Linux and will be worse on mobile; measure it on real devices before committing to the load strategy.

**11. Protect progress harder than the incumbents do, because save loss is a pedagogical failure.** SQL Island is stateless, SQLPD paywalled saving, and Database Detective can wipe saves with a Linux crash — all three are the same category of failure, and SQL Island's non-finishers stalling at **10, 15, 17 and 19 of 20 queries after an average 84 minutes** shows how much a single interruption costs. **Keep the Postgres data dir (large, rebuildable from the shipped snapshot) strictly separate from the progress record (tiny, irreplaceable)**, and never make completion history recoverable only by reading the Postgres DB, since a PGlite version bump should be able to discard and re-seed the data dir while preserving progress. Put small loss-tolerable flags in `localStorage` (practical cap **5 MiB per origin per storage type**) and everything substantial in IndexedDB. Call **`navigator.storage.persist()` after a real user gesture**, not on page load, because Safari and Chromium decide silently on interaction history — and surface the boolean result honestly in the UI. Then plan for the failure anyway: **Safari's ITP deletes script-written storage after seven days without user interaction, and whether `persist()` exempts an origin from that is undocumented and probably false.** Mitigate with a diegetic **"archive your case file" export/import** of a small versioned JSON envelope (deliberately not the ~5 MB snapshot), detection of a wiped DB on load with an import offer, and PWA install as the durable path. Version every persisted record with a `saveVersion` and an ordered array of pure migration functions.

**12. Keep SMM's frictionlessness, and treat accessibility and i18n as upstream design constraints.** No login, no payment, no server, one URL, a permissive licence: that is what put SMM in Dartmouth's CS 61, the Galaxy Training Network, ELIXIR's TeSS and public libraries, and it is why SQL Noir's Stripe gate — however well engineered its server-side answers are — forfeits exactly the distribution channel that made the genre. Keep the free tier genuinely complete. Adopt **SQL Noir's TypeScript-array-of-INSERTs case format** so a case is a reviewable text file that supports PR-based community authoring and per-locale forks, and ship a case template, contribution guide and test suite from day one — because the strongest competitive opening is not out-designing SMM but **out-maintaining it** against 15 open issues spanning seven years and two unfixed data bugs in its critical path. On accessibility, three WCAG 2.2 criteria determine architecture before any code: **2.5.7 makes a drag-only evidence board non-conformant**, so build a selection model from native `<button>` elements with drag layered on; **1.4.1 forbids colour-only correctness signals**, which the text verdict line satisfies for free; and **2.2.1 effectively bans timers on the critical path**, so all timing lives in an opt-in efficiency layer. Render results in a real `<table>` with a `role="status"` live region announcing row count and verdict on explicit run only, honour `prefers-reduced-motion` while ensuring the *information* never lives only in the motion, and design the focus ring deliberately (≥2 CSS px perimeter, ≥3:1 between states). Look up **2.5.8 Target Size's exact pixel value directly** — it was not verified here. For English plus Bahasa Indonesia, separate all strings into per-locale JSON keyed by stable ids (never by English text), use an `/id/` path prefix, set `<html lang>` correctly per page so screen readers pick the right voice, **keep SQL keywords, schema identifiers and the dataset untranslated while translating all narrative, UI and hint text** — translating table and column names forks every expected-answer query, which is exactly what the donated zh-CN SQL Noir fork did and why it is a separate schema tree — and add a build-time check that every `en` key exists in `id`.

## Conclusion

The competitive picture inverts the obvious assumption. SQL Murder Mystery's weaknesses are not oversights to be fixed one by one; they are the complete and predictable consequence of putting the grader inside the data file, which is also the source of the portability that made it the genre's default. SQL Noir has already spent its differentiation budget on that visible gap list and arrived at the same wall from the other side: it has cases, tiers, XP, locales, persistence and server-side answers, and it still cannot tell a player anything about the SQL they wrote. **The feature gap in this market has been closed; the diagnosis gap has not been touched.** That is why the strongest single number in the entire evidence base is not a star count or a review score but SQL-Tutor's **~1 standard deviation improvement from a single two-hour session** driven by 500+ constraints and no result-matching at all, alongside SQLab's finding that a richer diagnostic roughly doubled the error types it could detect. Gamification is the part of this corpus with the least extractable evidence and the most imitation; feedback specificity is the part with the most evidence and no competitor.

The second non-obvious conclusion is that the technology stopped being the constraint sometime in the last two years and nobody has noticed. A browser game can now execute **real PostgreSQL 18.6** with 36 of 36 tested features passing, including `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, FORMAT JSON)`, on a header-less static host, for roughly the gzipped weight of three high-resolution photographs — and parse that SQL with Postgres's own grammar for another 338 KB. Every incumbent runs SQLite, two of them pass raw engine errors straight through, and none of them teaches query plans because none of them can. **The practical risk to a new entrant is therefore not engineering and not pedagogy; it is authoring volume.** Golden Idol spent one to two months per scenario and never built a framework; SQL-Tutor needed 500+ hand-written constraints; SQLab's Sessform database carries over 500 feedback messages. A 60–100 level game with authored wrong answers on trap concepts, three independent clue paths per conclusion, and per-level diagnostic constraints is a content pipeline problem wearing a game-design costume — which is precisely why the computed-unlock DAG, the reviewable text-file case format, the build-time validators and the randomized-seed test harness are not polish items to defer. They are the only things that make the content volume survivable, and they are the difference between shipping the game that beats SQL Murder Mystery and shipping the fourteenth one that doesn't.
